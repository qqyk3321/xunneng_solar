SOLAR ENERGY AND ELECTRICITY

PV01
Q: What is solar energy?
A: Solar energy is energy arriving from the sun. Different technologies use it to produce electricity or heat. Rooftop photovoltaic panels generate electricity from light; they do not need to burn fuel. The available sunlight changes with location, season, time of day and weather.
Reference: S01

PV02
Q: Are photovoltaic panels and solar water heaters the same thing?
A: No. Photovoltaic, or PV, panels make electricity. Solar thermal equipment collects heat, for example to heat water. A home may have either or both, but their equipment and outputs differ. A PV system can power an electric water heater if the electrical system is designed for that load.
Reference: S01

PV03
Q: What are a solar cell, a module and an array?
A: A solar cell is a small device that converts light into electricity. Cells are assembled into a module, commonly called a panel. Multiple panels form an array. These words describe different levels of the generating equipment, not battery storage capacity.
Reference: S02

PV04
Q: What equipment is in a home solar system besides panels?
A: A complete system includes panels, mounting equipment, an inverter, electrical connections and protective equipment. A storage or backup design also needs compatible batteries and control equipment. The exact components depend on the roof, electricity supply and intended operation; a panel count alone does not describe a complete installation.
Reference: S02

PV05
Q: What are DC and AC, and why is an inverter needed?
A: Panels generate direct-current electricity, or DC. Ordinary household circuits use alternating current, or AC. An inverter performs the conversion. A battery also stores energy through DC electrical connections, so its usable connection to household circuits depends on compatible power-conversion equipment.
Reference: S19

PV06
Q: What do W, kW and kWp mean?
A: Watts and kilowatts measure power; 1 kW equals 1,000 W. Panel ratings are often described as peak power, written Wp or kWp, under defined test conditions. A system's power rating is different from how much energy it generates over a day or stores in a battery.
References: S03, S08

PV07
Q: What does kWh mean? Is it the same as kW?
A: kWh measures energy, while kW measures power. A steady 1 kW load running for 2 hours uses 2 kWh. Real appliances may vary their power while running, so actual energy use depends on their operating pattern as well as their rated power.
Reference: S03

PV08
Q: Will a panel always generate the wattage on its label?
A: No. The label is a rating under standard test conditions. Actual power changes with sunlight, panel temperature, orientation, shading and system conditions. For example, a 450 W panel is not a promise of 450 W throughout daylight hours. This example is not a Sol & Tide equipment specification.
Reference: S08

PV09
Q: Does twelve hours of daylight mean twelve hours at full solar output?
A: No. Sunlight is weaker around sunrise and sunset and varies during the day. Daily solar energy accounts for changing intensity over time. A generation estimate needs local sunlight data and system losses, rather than multiplying the panel rating by all daylight hours.
Reference: S01

PV10
Q: Can you tell daily solar generation from system size alone?
A: Not reliably. The same panel capacity can produce different daily energy at different sites. A useful estimate considers local sunlight, roof layout, shading, season and losses. Ask what assumptions an estimate uses; do not treat a sunny-day example as a guaranteed daily or annual yield.
Reference: S07

PV11
Q: Do solar panels generate on cloudy days? Do they work at night?
A: Panels can use diffuse daylight under cloud cover, usually with reduced output. The amount varies with conditions. At night they do not have useful sunlight for normal household generation; evening electricity must come from another source, such as previously stored battery energy or the grid.
Reference: S08

PV12
Q: Does hotter weather always mean more solar electricity?
A: No. Strong sunlight helps generation, but higher cell temperature generally reduces a panel's electrical conversion performance. A hot panel can produce less power than a cooler panel receiving the same light. System design should consider both sunlight and operating temperature.
Reference: S04

PV13
Q: Why does shading matter? Can one shaded panel affect others?
A: Shade reduces light reaching the affected cells. Its wider effect depends on the panel layout and electrical design. String arrangements, bypass devices and panel-level electronics respond differently. An installer should assess moving shade; it is inaccurate to promise either that one shadow disables everything or that shade never affects other panels.
References: S05, S06

ROOF, PANEL SELECTION AND SYSTEM SIZE

PV14
Q: Which direction should panels face?
A: The best layout depends on location, roof geometry, shading and when electricity is needed. East-facing areas tend to produce earlier and west-facing areas later. Do not automatically apply a roof-direction recommendation written for another hemisphere to Antigua or Dominica; assess the actual property.
Reference: S05

PV15
Q: Does roof angle matter? Can panels go on a flat roof?
A: Tilt affects sunlight exposure and layout. A flat or gently sloping roof may use an appropriate mounting arrangement, but row spacing, shading, wind loads and roof condition still need assessment. There is no universal tilt or mounting solution suitable for every home.
References: S05, S10

PV16
Q: How much roof space is needed, and does the roof's condition matter?
A: Available space is more than the visible roof area. Obstacles, shade, access and mounting requirements affect the layout. Panel dimensions vary. Roof condition and any near-term repairs should be reviewed before installation, along with whether the structure is suitable for the proposed system.
References: S05, S10

PV17
Q: How do I estimate an appliance's electricity consumption?
A: For a steady load, energy in kWh equals power in kW multiplied by hours. A hypothetical 100 W device used for 5 hours consumes 0.5 kWh. Cycling appliances need measured or representative average consumption. This arithmetic explains energy use; it does not by itself establish inverter or backup sizing.
Reference: S03

PV18
Q: What does panel efficiency mean? Is the highest percentage always the best choice?
A: Efficiency describes how much incoming light energy becomes electrical energy. Higher efficiency can help where roof space is limited, but the whole system's yield also depends on layout, temperature and losses. Compare output, usable roof area, equipment suitability and the proposal rather than one percentage.
Reference: S04

PV19
Q: Are all panels with the same wattage equivalent?
A: The power label alone is not a full comparison. Panels can differ in size, efficiency and performance under operating conditions. Check the proposed model's datasheet and warranty documents. A larger physical panel is not automatically more efficient, and a rated wattage does not establish suitability for your roof.
Reference: S08

PV20
Q: Should I put as many panels as possible on the roof?
A: Extra generation is useful when it matches consumption, storage or permitted export. It also adds cost and must fit the roof and inverter limits. A suitable design balances present and expected future use, available space, sunlight and budget. Bigger is not automatically the best value.
Reference: S07

INVERTERS AND ELECTRICITY FLOW

PV21
Q: Does a more powerful inverter automatically make the panels generate more?
A: No. An inverter converts the energy available from the panels; it does not create additional sunlight. Its rating limits how much AC power it can deliver. Panels, inverter and storage must be designed together, so a higher inverter rating alone does not establish higher daily generation.
References: S06, S07

PV22
Q: What is MPPT?
A: MPPT means Maximum Power Point Tracking. It adjusts the electrical operating point of a solar input to harvest useful power as conditions change. It may be built into an inverter or charge controller. It cannot replace missing sunlight, and allowable panel arrangements depend on the specific equipment.
Reference: S09

PV23
Q: How do string inverters, microinverters and optimisers differ?
A: A string inverter converts power from groups of panels. Microinverters convert power at individual panels. Optimisers provide panel-level DC control with a compatible inverter. These approaches differ in layout flexibility, shade response, monitoring and cost. No one type is automatically best for every property.
Reference: S19

PV24
Q: Why can panel capacity be larger than inverter capacity? What is clipping?
A: Panels do not always operate at their full rating, so a designed system may use more rated DC panel capacity than AC inverter capacity. If available power exceeds the inverter's conversion limit, output is capped; this is called clipping. Equipment input limits still apply, and the trade-off should be assessed rather than improvised.
Reference: S07

PV25
Q: Why is the generation shown in an app different from my electricity bill?
A: Solar generation, household consumption and grid imports are different measurements. Electricity used directly from solar does not appear as grid purchases. Monitoring may also show exports and battery flows. Compare the same dates and measurement boundaries; a difference alone does not prove a fault or billing error.
Reference: S13

PV26
Q: What is solar self-consumption?
A: Self-consumption means using solar electricity within the property, directly or after storage. Using suitable loads during sunny hours can reduce grid purchases. Savings depend on consumption and the tariff.
Reference: S14

PV27
Q: What happens to excess solar electricity? Will the utility pay me for it?
A: Excess may charge a battery, be exported or be curtailed, depending on the configuration and permission. Credit rates and connection conditions need local confirmation; another country's arrangements do not establish yours.
Reference: S14

PV28
Q: Does installing solar guarantee a zero electricity bill?
A: No. Grid imports and tariff charges may remain. Savings depend on the system, usage and local arrangements. Storage also has costs and losses, so neither zero bills nor a fixed payback is guaranteed.
Reference: S14

GRID CONNECTION, BACKUP AND OWNERSHIP

PV29
Q: Do panels lose performance as they age?
A: Panel output can gradually decline over time. A performance warranty sets model-specific conditions for warranted output; it is not a guarantee of a household's energy yield or bill savings. Ask for the proposed product's documents rather than assuming one degradation rate applies to every panel.
Reference: S11

PV30
Q: What is the difference between grid-connected, hybrid and off-grid solar?
A: Grid-connected systems operate alongside the utility. A hybrid design coordinates solar with storage and potentially other sources. Off-grid systems operate independently and must be designed around periods of low generation. These labels alone do not prove backup capability or indefinite energy independence.
References: S16, S06

PV31
Q: Why might solar stop when the electricity grid fails?
A: Ordinary grid-following solar generally shuts down during an outage to avoid supplying an uncontrolled island. Backup needs suitable equipment and a design that isolates supported circuits from the utility. A correctly configured solar-and-storage system can provide backup within its energy and power limits; solar panels alone are not a backup guarantee.
Reference: S17

PV32
Q: Is a panel's performance warranty the same as its product or installation warranty?
A: No. Product warranties address covered equipment defects, performance warranties address specified output conditions, and installation warranties concern covered workmanship. Coverage and exclusions differ. These general categories do not specify Sol & Tide's warranty; request the exact proposed terms in writing.
Reference: S11

PV33
Q: Are panels automatically suitable for hurricanes, coastal weather or any roof?
A: No universal suitability follows from calling a panel outdoor equipment. The selected equipment, mounting, roof and local site conditions need assessment. Ask the team for applicable model documents and the proposed structural approach. Do not infer a storm rating, corrosion guarantee or approval from general solar education.
Reference: X15; engineering suitability requires project assessment.

PV34
Q: Does a battery mean I no longer need enough solar panels?
A: A battery stores energy; it does not generate it. If the aim is to recharge it from solar while also powering daytime loads, generation must cover both within the available hours and charging limits. More storage alone cannot compensate for an ongoing shortage of energy input.
Reference: S18

PV35
Q: Are solar panels maintenance-free? Should I clean them myself?
A: Dirt, debris and faults can reduce performance, so follow the equipment's maintenance guidance and monitor output. Professional cleaning may be appropriate for the site. Arrange qualified help for roof or electrical work; general chat should not provide climbing, wiring or repair instructions.
Reference: S12

PV36
Q: What should I prepare before discussing a solar system?
A: Identify the property location, recent electricity use, daytime and evening routines, backup priorities and any expected new loads. Roof condition and available photos can help the discussion. These details support an assessment; they do not replace the site's engineering review or a final proposal.
References: S10, S15

STORAGE, POWER AND ENERGY

BAT01
Q: What is a home battery energy storage system, or BESS?
A: It stores electrical energy for later use, with battery cells, management and compatible power-conversion equipment. It can help move solar energy from daytime to evening or support backup when designed for that purpose. A storage system needs an energy source to recharge; it does not create electricity.
Reference: B01

BAT02
Q: Does a home battery require solar panels?
A: Not always. Some systems can charge from the grid without solar, if the equipment, settings and local arrangements allow it. A battery-only system still needs electricity to recharge. Whether this is a useful or available option for a particular project must be confirmed with the team.
Reference: B02

BAT03
Q: What is the difference between battery capacity in kWh and power in kW?
A: kWh describes stored energy; kW describes the rate of charging or discharging. Energy capacity affects how long a load may be supported, while power limits affect which loads can operate together. A high-capacity battery does not necessarily have high output power.
Reference: B03

BAT04
Q: What are nominal capacity and usable capacity?
A: Nominal capacity describes the battery's stated total energy capacity. Usable capacity is the amount made available under its operating limits. The difference depends on the product and settings. Check which figure a quotation uses before comparing batteries or estimating runtime.
Reference: B04

BAT05
Q: What does SOC mean?
A: State of charge, or SOC, estimates how full a battery is relative to its reference capacity, usually as a percentage. It is different from its power limit and long-term health. A displayed percentage alone does not tell you how many hours your household can run.
Reference: B03

BAT06
Q: What is depth of discharge, or DoD?
A: DoD describes how much battery capacity has been discharged relative to a reference full capacity. Deeper discharge uses more of the stored energy. Allowable operating ranges depend on the product; a generic DoD definition is not permission to change protection settings or fully empty a battery.
Reference: B03

BAT07
Q: What is state of health, or SOH? Is it the same as SOC?
A: SOC concerns the current charge level; SOH concerns the battery's condition relative to a reference new state. A battery can report full charge while holding less energy than when new. Health and available performance depend on the product's measurement method and operating history.
Reference: B05

BAT08
Q: What is a battery cycle? Does every small discharge count as a full cycle?
A: A cycle describes charging and discharging under defined conditions. A partial discharge is not necessarily one equivalent full cycle; the accounting method matters. When comparing a cycle-life claim, check the stated discharge depth, temperature and end-of-life criterion rather than comparing cycle numbers alone.
Reference: B06

BAT09
Q: What is battery energy throughput?
A: Throughput is the cumulative energy charged or delivered over time, according to the stated definition. Warranties may limit throughput as well as years or cycles. Check the exact terms, including which direction of energy flow is counted; throughput is not the battery's one-time capacity.
Reference: B07

BAT10
Q: Can a battery age even if it is not used every day?
A: Yes. Battery ageing includes time-related changes as well as changes associated with cycling. Storage conditions and use both matter. A low cycle count alone does not establish remaining life, and dividing a quoted cycle count by one cycle per day is not a guaranteed service-life calculation.
Reference: B08

BAT11
Q: What is round-trip efficiency? Why do charging and discharging lose energy?
A: Round-trip efficiency compares recovered energy with the energy put into storage over a defined cycle. Losses occur in the battery and conversion equipment. Results depend on the measurement boundary and conditions, so compare like-for-like figures and do not assume all charged energy reaches household loads.
Reference: B09

BAT12
Q: How many years will a battery last?
A: There is no universal lifetime for all home batteries. Check the model's warranty, retained-capacity condition, operating limits and any cycle or throughput limits. A warranty duration is not a promise of unchanged capacity or a precise replacement date. Sol & Tide's specific terms need confirmation in the proposal.
Reference: B10

CHEMISTRY, CONTROLS AND OPERATING MODES

BAT13
Q: What are LFP and LiFePO4? Is LFP a lithium-ion battery?
A: LFP stands for Lithium Iron Phosphate, also written LiFePO4. It is a lithium-ion battery chemistry used in stationary energy storage. Chemistry is only part of system selection: equipment quality, management, installation, operating limits and support still matter. Capacity and life are model-specific.
Reference: B11

BAT14
Q: Does an LFP battery eliminate all safety risk?
A: No battery chemistry eliminates all system risk. Appropriate design, protective controls, location and installation remain necessary. General comparisons of chemistry do not establish a particular product's certification or make it suitable for any indoor or outdoor location. Request the relevant equipment and installation documentation.
Reference: B11

BAT15
Q: What does a battery management system, or BMS, do?
A: A BMS monitors battery operating conditions and helps enforce protection limits, such as permitted charging and discharging. Its functions and communication with the inverter are product-specific. It is not a substitute for a compatible installation, and customers should not bypass it or change protection thresholds through general chat advice.
Reference: B12

BAT16
Q: What is C-rate? Can temperature affect battery power?
A: C-rate expresses a charge or discharge rate relative to battery capacity. Higher current and operating temperature can affect available performance and limits. Use the manufacturer's specifications for the actual unit; a chemistry name or energy-capacity figure does not establish permissible charging power or temperature limits.
Reference: B05

BAT17
Q: What is a hybrid inverter? Does that name guarantee backup?
A: A hybrid inverter coordinates solar and battery conversion in a compatible design. Backup also depends on isolation, control equipment, wiring and the supported circuits. Do not infer whole-house backup, generator support or uninterrupted transfer from the word hybrid alone; confirm the exact system functions.
Reference: B13

BAT18
Q: What do AC-coupled and DC-coupled storage mean?
A: With AC coupling, the solar and storage systems connect through the AC side, with conversion equipment for each. With DC coupling, solar and storage share a DC-side arrangement with suitable conversion equipment. These are design approaches, not universal rankings of quality or compatibility. The installation must suit the equipment and site.
Reference: B04

BAT19
Q: Can I add a battery to solar I already own?
A: Compatibility needs assessment of the existing inverter, controls, electrical layout and backup requirements. The team should review the current equipment before recommending an upgrade. A matching brand or voltage alone does not confirm compatibility.
Reference: B14

BAT20
Q: Can a battery charge from the grid as well as solar?
A: Some systems support both, subject to equipment, permitted settings and local arrangements. Grid charging consumes purchased electricity and has losses. It should not be presented as free energy or an automatic savings feature. Ask the team to confirm availability and the purpose of the proposed operating mode.
Reference: B02

BAT21
Q: What does self-consumption or self-powered battery mode do?
A: In a supported configuration, surplus solar charges the battery for later household use, reducing grid imports. The system's reserve, available charge and power limits still apply. Exact priorities and export behaviour depend on the selected equipment and settings; this is not proof that the home becomes off-grid.
Reference: B15

BAT22
Q: What is time-of-use optimisation? Is it useful with every electricity tariff?
A: A supported system may shift charging and use between differently priced periods. Benefits depend on the actual tariff, losses and costs. A foreign time-of-use example does not establish an offer in Antigua or Dominica.
Reference: B16

BAT23
Q: What is a backup reserve? Should it always be set to the maximum?
A: A reserve can hold energy for outages rather than routine use. A higher reserve can leave more energy available if an outage starts, but may reduce energy used for everyday savings. Supported settings and outage behaviour are model-specific; agree the balance with the installer rather than assuming one percentage suits every home.
Reference: B17

BACKUP, APPLIANCES AND RUNTIME

BAT24
Q: Does adding any battery guarantee power during an outage?

G01 — Electrical quantities: reading power, energy, voltage and apparent power
Related topics: W, kW, kWh, V, A, Ah, VA, kVA, power factor; power, energy, volts, amps, ampere-hours, apparent power

FOUNDATION
Power is a rate; energy accumulates over time. Voltage and current describe different electrical quantities. In AC equipment, real-power and apparent-power limits may differ, so a kW rating and a kVA rating should not be assumed interchangeable. Power factor relates real power to apparent power under the stated conditions. [D01]

EXPLANATION AND CUSTOMER APPLICATION
Read a number together with its unit and its purpose. A panel's kW rating describes a generating capability at reference conditions; an inverter's kW describes a conversion or output limit; a battery's kWh describes stored energy at a defined boundary. These can all appear on the same proposal while answering different questions. Converting all of them into a single 'system size' loses important information.

For a constant load, energy equals power multiplied by elapsed time. If power varies, add the energy in successive intervals or use a representative measured total. Nameplate input may describe a maximum or test condition rather than a continuously drawn load. A refrigerator, air conditioner and water pump can each spend time in different operating states.

Amp-hours describe charge, not complete household energy. Nominal volts multiplied by amp-hours can provide an approximate nominal Wh figure. The result needs the correct battery or bank boundary: do not use one cell's voltage with the entire bank's claimed Ah, or treat a nominal DC figure as usable AC output. Voltage varies and the equipment specification remains the reference.

Apparent power is relevant when reviewing equipment that carries separate VA and W limits. If a hypothetical load has real power of 1.2 kW and power factor 0.8, the corresponding apparent power is 1.5 kVA. That arithmetic is an explanation, not a recommendation for a customer's circuit or UPS. Starting behaviour, harmonics and equipment limits still need professional assessment.

A good customer explanation keeps these layers separate: how much power is needed at once, how much energy is needed over a period, and which supply and equipment characteristics must be compatible. This lets the customer describe a goal without being asked to perform electrical measurements or expose wiring.

WHAT TO CLARIFY
Ask for a model label or manufacturer specification where available, the operating hours and which loads run together. Never ask the customer to open an electrical enclosure to obtain a reading.

SUMMARY
Start with the units and the equipment being described. kW measures power at a moment; kWh measures energy over time. Ah cannot be treated as usable household energy without voltage and other relevant information. Battery energy capacity, inverter output and panel rated power answer different questions. For equipment rated in kVA, distinguish real power from apparent power. The assistant can explain ratings; engineers must confirm electrical compatibility.

G02 — Solar resource: daylight, irradiance, daily energy and weather uncertainty
Related topics: sun hours, irradiance, insolation, peak sun hours, seasons, forecast; daylight, cloudy conditions, seasonal variation

FOUNDATION
Solar-performance models distinguish weather inputs, the light reaching the panel plane and the conditions influencing electrical output. A measurement at one instant is different from energy integrated across a day. Effective irradiance further accounts for optical effects such as soiling and angle of incidence. [D02, D03]

EXPLANATION AND CUSTOMER APPLICATION
Day length is not a promise of rated generation throughout those hours. Morning, midday and late afternoon can have very different light intensity. A rooftop may also move into and out of shade. This is why a useful energy forecast is based on a time profile rather than panel capacity multiplied by every daylight hour.

Equivalent full-sun hours compress a changing daily light profile into an energy-equivalent number. They do not mean that the roof receives an unchanging peak intensity for that exact continuous period. When using an illustrative number in a conversation, label it as an assumption rather than a measured fact about the customer's island or roof.

Distinguish location-level resource from site-level access. A sunny region can still contain a deeply shaded roof. Nearby structures, trees, roof orientation and the usable installation area affect what the proposed array receives. A regional forecast does not substitute for a layout assessment.

An annual average is useful for planning but cannot describe every day, month or outage event. A design chosen for average bill reduction may need a different reserve or backup strategy for several weak-sun days. Weather-year data, actual future weather and a single forecast are different kinds of evidence.

A practical conversation starts with the customer's objective. Someone mainly concerned with bill reduction may want an annual estimate and its assumptions. Someone worried about long outages needs an explanation of low-generation periods, storage and any supported additional energy source. The assistant should not turn a regional sunshine statement into an autonomy guarantee.

WHAT TO CLARIFY
Ask which period and objective the customer is discussing: an instant, a typical day, an annual estimate or a specific outage. Use local site information before giving a project forecast.

SUMMARY
Daylight duration, irradiance and daily solar energy are different quantities. Peak sun hours are an equivalent-energy measure, not a promise of fixed hours at full output every day. A sunny region does not prove that a particular roof is unshaded. Annual generation estimates and backup through consecutive cloudy days require separate analysis. Do not promise a fixed yield without appropriate roof and weather data.

G03 — Photovoltaic cells and module technologies: understanding comparisons
Related topics: silicon, monocrystalline, polycrystalline, thin film, bifacial, tandem; module efficiency, cell technology

FOUNDATION
Photovoltaic cells use semiconductor structures to convert light into electrical energy. Crystalline silicon and thin-film technologies are broad families. Bifacial collection and tandem structures describe different design features; a technology label alone is not a complete system specification. [D04]

EXPLANATION AND CUSTOMER APPLICATION
A cell is the converting element; a module packages many cells into an outdoor generating product. The resulting module still needs appropriate electrical conversion, mounting and system controls. A strong laboratory cell result does not establish a particular rooftop product's dimensions, price, availability or lifetime performance.

Customers often hear several labels at once and assume they are competing descriptions of the same property. Some refer to the semiconductor material, some to how the cell is constructed, some to how light is collected, and others to module packaging. Ask for the exact proposed model rather than inferring every feature from one marketing term.

Compare products at the level that matters to the roof. Relevant information includes rated output, physical size, efficiency, temperature behaviour, applicable tests and the terms covering equipment and output. On a constrained roof, power per available area may matter; on another project, layout or shading can dominate the decision.

A bifacial module can only benefit from light that actually reaches its rear. Roof colour, clearance and mounting geometry influence that opportunity. 'Two sides' is not an instruction to double the estimate. Likewise, a research report about a tandem cell is not proof that a quoted module uses that technology or is available through Sol & Tide.

A useful response to 'Which technology is best?' is to identify the comparison goal. Is the customer short of roof area, concerned about hot operating conditions, trying to fit an unusual roof, or comparing warranty documents? The assistant can explain those criteria and direct the model-specific choice to the proposal.

WHAT TO CLARIFY
Use the company's confirmed equipment information for offered products. Treat external manufacturers and research examples as educational references, not an approved supplier list.

SUMMARY
Panel terminology may describe the material, cell structure, module construction or method of collecting light. One technology label cannot establish the quality of an entire system. Laboratory cell efficiency is not the same as the benefit of a rooftop module. Compare actual models, output, dimensions, temperature behavior, test documentation and warranty terms. Bifacial modules need useful rear-side light; they do not automatically double generation.

G04 — Reading a panel datasheet without turning it into a wiring plan
Related topics: Pmax, Voc, Isc, Vmp, Imp, STC, NMOT, temperature coefficient; open-circuit voltage, short-circuit current, maximum power point, standard test conditions

FOUNDATION
A module's current-voltage relationship includes short-circuit current, open-circuit voltage and a maximum-power operating point. These quantities vary with conditions, including irradiance and cell temperature. A datasheet's reference conditions must therefore accompany its numerical values. [D05]

EXPLANATION AND CUSTOMER APPLICATION
Pmax is a power rating at stated conditions. Vmp and Imp describe voltage and current at the maximum-power operating point; their product is associated with that power. Voc describes open-circuit voltage, while Isc describes short-circuit current under the relevant conditions. These are not interchangeable versions of one operating current or voltage.

A customer reading the largest number on each row may accidentally combine values that do not occur together. Multiplying open-circuit voltage by short-circuit current does not describe the module's normal useful output. The operating point lies on a relationship between current and voltage, not a menu from which maximum values can be independently selected.

Test-condition ratings help make controlled comparisons. Operating conditions on a real roof differ. A hot sunny afternoon, a cooler bright period and a cloudy morning can each produce a different electrical state. Where a datasheet offers additional operating-temperature or performance information, ask the designer how it was used in the site estimate.

Temperature coefficients indicate a direction and scale of change for a specified parameter and reference. Do not apply a power coefficient to voltage or a voltage coefficient to energy without the appropriate model. A simplified example can teach sensitivity, but it is not a final performance calculation.

Input-voltage, current and MPPT constraints belong to the complete design. The support assistant may explain what these headings mean, but should not instruct a visitor to choose a string length, rearrange connectors or override an inverter limit. The model, site extremes and professional design all matter.

WHAT TO CLARIFY
For a model-specific question, request the published model designation or datasheet through an appropriate channel. Do not infer compatibility from wattage alone or direct a customer to make open-circuit or short-circuit tests.

SUMMARY
Pmax, Vmp, Imp, Voc and Isc describe different operating conditions. Do not multiply unrelated maximum values and present the result as normal operating power. Understand ratings together with their test conditions; roof operation depends on irradiance and cell temperature. Explain the terms without giving customers instructions for string wiring, short-circuit current measurements or electrical modifications.

G05 — Inverters, MPPT, conversion limits and the meaning of compatible operation
Related topics: inverter, MPPT, clipping, grid following, grid forming, hybrid; power limiting, grid connection, grid-forming operation

FOUNDATION
MPPT is a control function that seeks a useful maximum-power operating point as conditions change. The array, converter and AC output each have limits. A control function does not create sunlight or remove those limits. [D06]

EXPLANATION AND CUSTOMER APPLICATION
Think of the inverter as part of a conversion and control chain. It must work with the available solar input, supported battery arrangement and the household or grid interface. The nameplate output is important, but it is not a complete description of charging limits, backup coverage, generator behaviour or transfer performance.

Different functions can be combined into one enclosure or provided by separate devices. This changes neither the need to check compatibility nor the need to identify each function. A hybrid label may indicate coordinated solar and battery operation, but a proposal still needs to state what happens in grid-connected operation and during an outage.

An inverter can limit output for more than one reason. A conversion ceiling, battery charging limit, export constraint, selected mode or protective operating condition may all influence measured output. A flat section on a graph alone does not establish which condition is responsible. Ask for context before naming it a fault.

DC panel capacity and AC conversion capacity need not match numerically. A designer may balance better use during lower-light periods against some capped output during strong conditions. That trade-off is different from exceeding a manufacturer's allowed input range. The assistant can explain the distinction without recommending equipment changes.

When a visitor compares two inverters, organise the question around the intended job: required simultaneous load, battery interface, desired backup circuits, supply arrangement, supported monitoring and future plans. A higher headline kW rating is useful only if the rest of the proposed system and site can use it.

WHAT TO CLARIFY
Keep the distinction between a general operating concept and a verified capability of a specific model. Refer unusual source-combination or backup questions to the project team.

SUMMARY
An inverter converts energy and coordinates operation with panels, batteries, the grid and loads. MPPT helps find an appropriate operating point but does not create additional solar energy. Limited output on a graph can have several causes and does not, by itself, prove a fault. The label 'hybrid inverter' does not replace confirmation of backup circuits, transfer performance and equipment compatibility.

G06 — Site assessment: the roof, usable space, access and equipment location
Related topics: roof assessment, renovation, flat roof, access, battery location; structural condition, usable installation space

FOUNDATION
Professional assessment considers the actual site, not only the desired capacity. Layout, installation access, roof condition and equipment location can affect design and scope. This guide describes information to discuss, not a structural certificate or local installation rule. [D07]

EXPLANATION AND CUSTOMER APPLICATION
Separate gross area from usable area. A large roof can contain obstacles, incompatible surfaces, shaded sections and access needs. The useful layout is not obtained by dividing roof area by one panel's dimensions and ignoring everything between them. Two properties with the same floor area can support very different layouts.

Roof condition is a lifecycle issue. If substantial work is already planned, installation timing should be coordinated so that removal and reinstatement are considered early. The assistant should not promise that roof work is included, that every roof can carry the equipment, or that one photograph settles structural suitability.

Equipment space has several dimensions: physical fit, environmental suitability, permitted access and how the proposed system connects with the property's infrastructure. A battery's footprint is not the same as the complete installation envelope. A sheltered-looking location also needs model-specific assessment.

Photos can help the team understand a site, but do not request unsafe access. Useful images are those the customer can share without climbing or opening equipment. State what the image is intended to show, such as the overall roof or the proposed ground-level area. Do not treat an image as proof of hidden structure or wiring.

Present unknown site details as questions for the assessment, not obstacles that the assistant has already diagnosed. A concise enquiry might establish the island, whether the building is existing or under construction, the main goal and whether relevant photos are available. The project team can determine which additional information is needed.

WHAT TO CLARIFY
Ask what is known, record what remains unverified, and keep preliminary discussions separate from a final design. Local requirements and model-specific location limits need confirmation.

SUMMARY
A site assessment considers shading, structural condition, maintenance access, planned roof work and the equipment environment as well as roof area. Battery dimensions alone do not define the required installation space. Photographs can provide context but cannot establish hidden structural or electrical conditions. Do not ask customers to climb onto a roof or open equipment to gather evidence.

G07 — Battery construction, chemistry and protective control layers
Related topics: cell, module, pack, LFP, NMC, BMS, EMS, cell balancing; lithium iron phosphate, battery management

FOUNDATION
Battery cells, their managed grouping and system controls perform different jobs. A battery management system monitors and constrains battery operation, while wider controls coordinate energy use. A favourable chemistry description is not a complete safety or compatibility evaluation. [D08]

EXPLANATION AND CUSTOMER APPLICATION
An individual cell is not the whole home-storage product. Cells may be assembled into modules and a larger pack or bank with monitoring, protection, enclosures and interfaces. A comparison that quotes a cell's laboratory characteristic may not describe energy delivered by the installed system at household circuits.

Chemistry names are useful categories, but customers usually need an application-level comparison. Ask which documented usable energy, output limits, operating environment, support and warranty apply to the proposed product. Avoid a universal statement that one chemistry never ages or removes every risk.

Protective controls are not optional conveniences. A limit on charging or discharging may be related to conditions reported by the battery and the rest of the system. The fact that a customer wants more output does not establish that a protective limit is unnecessary. General support should not suggest defeating it.

Balancing and battery-state estimation are different ideas. Keeping cells within their intended operating relationship does not mean every displayed charge percentage must be exact at every instant. Equally, a changed percentage alone cannot identify a failed cell or justify a replacement recommendation from the chatbot.

Where equipment is modular, expansion still needs a supported combination. Communication, firmware compatibility, permissible configuration and manufacturer requirements matter alongside nominal capacity and voltage. The assistant can help the customer gather model information while leaving the actual compatibility decision to the team.

WHAT TO CLARIFY
For chemistry or BMS questions, explain the role of the component and use confirmed product documents for actual limits. Do not name a control threshold, chemistry advantage or compatibility promise that has not been verified.

SUMMARY
Cells, modules, battery packs and complete home storage systems are different levels of equipment. Chemistry is one classification; protection, control, output capability, environment, warranty and support also matter. A BMS provides protective management, while system-level energy scheduling has a separate role. Do not recommend bypassing protection or mixing unconfirmed batteries to obtain more power.

G08 — Storage performance over time: SOC, SOH, cycles, throughput and reserve
Related topics: SOC, SOH, DoD, battery ageing, calendar life, cycle life; state of charge, state of health, depth of discharge, degradation

FOUNDATION
Calendar ageing and cycling describe different influences on storage life. Temperature, state of charge and operating profile can affect the result. Years, cycles and cumulative energy are therefore different ways to describe a battery's use and limitations. [D09]

EXPLANATION AND CUSTOMER APPLICATION
SOC describes the present charge estimate relative to a reference. SOH describes condition relative to another reference, often an earlier or nominal state. A fully charged older battery can therefore hold less energy than when it was new. Neither percentage by itself states the permitted discharge power.

Depth of discharge describes how much of a reference capacity has been used. An equivalent full-cycle measure aggregates partial use according to a defined method; it is not necessarily a counter incremented every time a charger starts. When reviewing a lifecycle figure, ask about its conditions and endpoint.

Throughput concerns accumulated energy over many operations. A warranty can use years, throughput, cycles or a combination. Compare the applicable written terms rather than dividing a laboratory cycle figure by the number of calendar days and reporting the result as a guaranteed service life.

Backup reserve and usable capacity need clear boundaries. A reserve held for a future outage may reduce routine daily discharge, but it is not the same as a product's permanent inaccessible capacity. Settings and outage behaviour differ. Explain the purpose of the reserve before discussing a customer preference.

Health estimates are useful context, not a replacement for support evaluation. If a customer reports an unexpected change, gather the visible observation and circumstances through the appropriate channel. Do not prescribe calibration cycles, deeply discharge a battery or reset a monitor in order to make a percentage look right.

WHAT TO CLARIFY
Clarify which quantity is being compared, the reference capacity, and whether the question is about present runtime, routine operation or warranty conditions.

SUMMARY
SOC estimates the present charge level, SOH describes health relative to a reference, and DoD describes the discharged proportion. Cycles, elapsed years and energy throughput have different definitions and are not interchangeable. A backup reserve is also different from permanently unavailable capacity. Record unusual readings and refer them to support; do not ask customers to deep-discharge or reset a system for calibration.

G09 — Energy-management modes and the trade-offs between savings and resilience
Related topics: self-consumption, reserve, time of use, grid charge, export control; backup reserve, grid charging

FOUNDATION
Energy-management controls decide when supported sources and storage contribute. The available modes depend on the selected equipment and arrangements. The word optimisation does not establish a local tariff, external control programme or free-energy opportunity. [Company scope and approved foundations]

EXPLANATION AND CUSTOMER APPLICATION
Self-consumption aims to use more generated energy within the property. The system may use solar directly, charge storage with a surplus and discharge later within its limits. This is an energy-flow strategy; the financial result still depends on the tariff, costs and what would otherwise happen to the energy.

Backup reserve prioritises a later event that may or may not occur. Holding more energy can improve preparedness when an outage begins, while leaving less storage available for daily shifting. Discuss this as a customer objective rather than declaring one percentage correct for all households.

Time-based operation only has its intended economic meaning when the relevant prices and permissions exist. A system might be technically able to charge from the grid but have no attractive price difference to exploit. Losses, usage changes and purchase conditions belong in the comparison.

Export limits or low demand can influence how much solar is used at a moment. If a battery is full and household demand is small, unused generating potential may be limited rather than visibly produced and discarded. A low app value therefore needs context before it is called a hardware failure.

Automatic modes should be understood through their documented priorities. Ask the installer to explain normal operation, outage operation and low-energy behaviour. The assistant can translate those concepts into plain language, but cannot confirm a live setting or authorise a parameter change without the actual model and support process.

WHAT TO CLARIFY
Separate the customer's goal from the setting they propose. Ask whether the priority is daily savings, selected backup loads or readiness for longer interruptions.

SUMMARY
The same battery can support daily self-consumption or retain more energy for an outage. Keeping a larger reserve creates a trade-off with daily usable energy. Time-of-use strategies require an applicable tariff and supported equipment functions. Grid charging is not free energy. Clarify the customer's objective before explaining modes, and do not change equipment settings on the customer's behalf.

G10 — System architectures: AC/DC coupling, backup boundaries and additional sources
Related topics: AC coupling, DC coupling, essential loads, whole house, generator, phase, restart; backup circuits, phase count, restart after depletion

FOUNDATION
The location of conversion stages and control equipment determines how sources interact. A backup system also needs an appropriate separation from the utility and support for its intended circuits. A diagram that includes all devices does not by itself establish a supported operating arrangement. [Approved architecture foundations]

EXPLANATION AND CUSTOMER APPLICATION
AC-coupled and DC-coupled describe connection approaches at a system level. Either may be relevant to a particular project, especially where solar already exists. The useful comparison is the supported energy path, conversion, controls and integration scope, not a claim that one acronym is universally superior.

An existing installation adds constraints that a new design may not have. Identify the present inverter and battery equipment, the customer's desired change and any backup requirement. Do not assume a new battery can be attached merely because its nominal voltage appears compatible.

Backup has a boundary. Essential-load arrangements identify selected circuits; wider arrangements still have energy, power and phase-dependent limits. Ask the proposal to explain what is inside that boundary and what happens when several large appliances request power together.

Generator integration is another compatibility question, not a default accessory. The generator, inverter, controls and electrical arrangement need review. Sol & Tide can assess the question according to its published service information, but the assistant cannot approve every generator or provide connection instructions.

Restart and transfer are separate behaviours. The speed of switching from a healthy grid does not establish what happens after low-energy shutdown, and neither establishes uninterrupted service to every sensitive load. Ask for the documented behaviour that matches the customer's concern rather than using a broad 'backup' label.

WHAT TO CLARIFY
A useful architecture enquiry states what is already installed, which sources are intended, which circuits matter and the required operating situations. Detailed design and connection work remain professional tasks.

SUMMARY
AC and DC coupling describe connection and conversion arrangements, rather than a universal ranking of system quality. Backup scope must identify supported circuits, phases and combinations of loads. Generator compatibility, transfer time and restart after depletion are separate capabilities that require confirmation. A 'hybrid' label does not establish that every one of these capabilities is available.

G11 — Household load profiles: appliances, coincidence and daily routines
Related topics: load profile, air conditioning, refrigerator, cooking, pump, EV; household consumption, appliance demand

FOUNDATION
A useful load description distinguishes input power, accumulated energy and the time of use. Cooling output is not electrical input, and intermittent or variable-speed operation makes a nameplate-only energy estimate uncertain. [D10]

EXPLANATION AND CUSTOMER APPLICATION
Build a load picture around activities. Breakfast, work, cooling, laundry and evening cooking create different combinations. A monthly total hides those combinations. Two homes using the same monthly kWh may need different solar-to-storage balances because their demand occurs at different times.

Air conditioning requires more than a count of units or their cooling ratings. Relevant information includes the actual electrical characteristics, expected operating hours, cycling or variable operation and whether units start or run together. The question 'Can it run an air conditioner?' must therefore be narrowed to a meaningful operating scenario.

Refrigeration is often an important backup priority, but its compressor does not necessarily operate continuously at a single power level. A documented or measured energy total helps with the energy side; starting capability still needs a separate check. Do not turn a yearly label average into a guarantee for every hot day or outage.

Short-duration appliances can matter to output power even when their daily energy is modest. Conversely, a modest continuous load can become significant across many hours. Compare both dimensions before deciding which appliances are optional during backup.

Future loads belong in the discussion. An EV, additional cooling equipment or a new household routine can change the demand profile. The assistant should record these plans as inputs for the team, not automatically enlarge every package or promise that existing wiring and equipment can accommodate them.

WHAT TO CLARIFY
Use a simple list: appliance or activity, input power if known, desired operating period, must-run priority, and simultaneous-use notes. Avoid requesting unsafe measurements or a lengthy compulsory intake before answering basic questions.

SUMMARY
Assess household demand by power, duration, timing and simultaneous use. Air-conditioning cooling capacity is not its electrical input power; a refrigerator's average energy consumption does not establish its starting demand. Brief high-power loads and small continuous loads both affect sizing. Tell the team about planned electric vehicles, additional air conditioning and changes in household routines.

G12 — A sizing method: objectives, constraints and scenario comparison
Related topics: system sizing, battery sizing, budget, roof limit, essential loads; backup duration, design constraints

FOUNDATION
Project sizing combines a generating profile, load profile, storage behaviour and equipment constraints. No single number, such as room count, monthly spend or battery capacity, establishes all of these. The workflow below is an original customer-facing planning framework.

EXPLANATION AND CUSTOMER APPLICATION
Start with the objective. Reducing routine grid purchases, supporting essentials during an outage and aiming for longer autonomy are related but different goals. State which is primary and what level of performance the customer hopes to discuss. Avoid assuming a budget enquiry means whole-house outage protection.

Then separate the constraints: usable installation area, site conditions, electrical compatibility, required simultaneous power, energy required over the target period and budget. Some constraints cannot be exchanged for one another. More stored energy, for example, does not remove a converter power ceiling.

Compare scenarios with the same assumptions. A basic comparison might hold the essential loads and target hours constant while changing storage. Another might hold equipment constant while distinguishing daytime-heavy and evening-heavy use. If both equipment and assumptions change, explain which change drives the result.

Treat average use and difficult conditions explicitly. An annual energy balance can look favourable while a particular evening or weak-sun period still requires grid energy. Outage planning also depends on the charge available when the outage begins. State the starting conditions before presenting a result.

Translate the comparison into questions for the proposal. Which loads are included? What energy and power boundary is being quoted? Which scope is confirmed and which site details remain uncertain? The assistant's role is to prepare a clearer assessment conversation, not to produce a final engineering design from incomplete chat information.

WHAT TO CLARIFY
When a customer asks for a recommendation, identify the missing fact with the greatest effect and ask one focused question. Use a package as a discussion starting point, not as a confirmed fit.

SUMMARY
Define the objective, list the constraints and then compare options using consistent assumptions. Bill savings, essential-load backup and prolonged off-grid operation are different objectives. Roof area, simultaneous power, required energy and budget impose different constraints. The assistant can structure the comparison, but final equipment selection requires site and product information.

G13 — Calculation discipline: units, boundaries, assumptions and uncertainty
Related topics: calculate runtime, battery charging, solar energy, losses, reserves; runtime, charging losses, assumptions

FOUNDATION
Energy accounting requires consistent units, time periods and measurement boundaries. Calculations in this chapter are original hypothetical teaching examples. They are not measured local data, a Sol & Tide configuration or an installation recommendation.

EXPLANATION AND CUSTOMER APPLICATION
Define what each input represents before multiplying it. Is battery energy nominal DC capacity, usable DC capacity or energy actually available at the load? Is a power figure rated, measured at a moment or averaged over a period? These distinctions often matter more than adding more decimal places.

For a constant load, E = P × t. If power changes, add the energy of the separate periods. A load using 0.4 kW for three hours and 0.8 kW for two hours consumes 2.8 kWh in that simplified profile. The calculation does not include unspecified appliances or conversion losses.

Runtime = available delivered energy / average load is a useful teaching relationship. It does not prove that the output equipment can start or sustain the load. Check the power constraint separately. A large energy figure can coexist with insufficient instantaneous power.

Apply a loss or reserve allowance once, at the boundary for which it is defined. If an input is already usable delivered energy, subtracting the same allowance again understates it. If a quoted figure is nominal, ignoring a relevant limit can overstate it. State uncertainty when the boundary is not known.

Charging time needs net charging power, not array nameplate capacity. Household demand, available sunlight and the charging limit may constrain it. A five-hour arithmetic result at constant charging power does not mean every cloudy day will replenish the battery in five clock hours.

Present scenarios rather than false precision. Changing demand, starting SOC, reserve or available solar can each alter the result. Where important inputs are unknown, explain the relationship and ask the team to confirm the actual values instead of fabricating a precise backup duration.

WHAT TO CLARIFY
Every numerical answer should state its inputs and assumptions, distinguish hypothetical from actual data, and mention any separate power constraint relevant to the question.

SUMMARY
Every calculation needs clear units and boundaries. Do not interchange rated values, instantaneous measurements and averages, or nominal battery energy and usable energy at the load. Avoid counting losses or reserves twice. Check power capability separately from runtime, and calculate charging from the net power remaining after loads. Teaching examples are not evidence of a customer's actual system performance.

G14 — Electricity bills and project economics: a transparent comparison
Related topics: bill, tariff, payback, return, fixed charge, export, finance; electricity price, export value, financing

FOUNDATION
Simple payback is a limited comparison that does not include the time value of money. Wider economic comparisons may account for future cash flows and costs. Storage economics also depend on energy throughput, replacement and operating assumptions. [D11, D12]

EXPLANATION AND CUSTOMER APPLICATION
Begin with the bill boundary. Grid energy, fixed charges, taxes or unrelated services are not interchangeable inputs. A solar system can change one part without eliminating the whole invoice. The company calculator is a planning aid with stated assumptions, not a live tariff feed or a substitute for a proposal.

Distinguish the financial use of each unit of solar energy. Energy used directly, energy shifted through a battery and energy exported may have different values. A stored kWh is not automatically an additional free kWh, because charging, losses and the alternative use of the energy matter.

Compare alternatives incrementally. If a battery is added to a solar proposal, compare the extra battery cost with the extra benefit it creates rather than attributing all solar savings to the battery. At the same time, acknowledge that outage preparedness can have value the customer does not measure only through a bill.

Use internally consistent hypothetical values when explaining arithmetic. A project cost of 20,000 currency units and annual net savings of 2,000 would give a ten-year simple payback under a constant-savings assumption. Those numbers are not local prices or a forecast, and the calculation omits financing and changing conditions unless explicitly added.

Ask about uncertainty before reporting a return. Tariffs, weather, usage, maintenance and replacement can change outcomes. A more detailed model also needs a stated period and financial assumptions. The assistant should explain what is missing rather than inventing a discount rate, finance offer or guaranteed return.

WHAT TO CLARIFY
For a personal purchase decision, use the correct regional calculator and a tailored proposal. Do not transplant a foreign subsidy, export credit, time-of-use tariff or financing term into company advice.

SUMMARY
Separate the components of the electricity bill and distinguish the value of direct solar use, stored energy used later and exported energy. Evaluate a battery's additional cost against its additional benefit; do not attribute all solar savings to the battery. Simple payback is not a complete investment analysis or a guarantee of returns. Backup value can be discussed separately.

G15 — Monitoring literacy: what the dashboard can and cannot prove
Related topics: monitoring, graph, kW, kWh, import, export, SOC, timestamp; energy monitoring, grid imports, self-consumption

FOUNDATION
Monitoring systems can report different quantities at different boundaries and update intervals. A local display, an inverter portal and the utility bill need not show the same thing. Battery-state and runtime displays also depend on the product's estimation method and available data. [D13]

EXPLANATION AND CUSTOMER APPLICATION
Read the metric name before interpreting the number. Generation is not household consumption; grid import is not all energy used; export is not all unused sunlight; battery charging power is not its stored capacity. Comparing unrelated metrics can create the appearance of a problem when the readings are simply about different flows.

Align time periods. A daily total, a live reading and a billing-period total cannot be directly compared. Also check whether a graph is cumulative energy or sampled power, and whether its timestamps and timezone match the period the customer is discussing.

A power-flow diagram is a simplified representation. Devices may update at different moments, and some data may be estimated. Small or transient discrepancies require context. A screenshot alone is not proof of faulty metering, an electrical defect or unauthorised energy use.

Treat app savings as an estimate with tariff and measurement assumptions. The utility bill can include charges the app does not model, and it may not show all behind-the-meter use. Ask which values and dates are being compared before concluding that expected savings have disappeared.

When collecting a support report, ask for observable facts: what changed, when it happened, which devices or readings were affected and whether an alert is shown. Use a supported channel for relevant screenshots. Passwords, private access links and unnecessary account information are not required for a general explanation.

WHAT TO CLARIFY
The assistant cannot log into equipment or inspect live telemetry in this setup. Interpret the concept, record the observation and direct case-specific verification to support.

SUMMARY
Read monitoring data with its metric name, time range, unit and measurement boundary. Solar generation is not total household consumption, and grid imports are not the household's only energy source. Instantaneous power, daily energy totals and billing-period figures cannot be compared without aligning their scope. A screenshot can provide clues but cannot prove that a component has failed or that a utility meter is inaccurate.

Professional operating and maintenance practices consider performance, condition, documentation and site risks over time. Gather observable facts through a supported contact channel, respect the equipment instructions and escalate case-specific decisions. Do not provide enclosure-opening, disconnection, protection-bypass or repair steps.

SUMMARY
Describe the observed behavior before assigning a cause or deciding whether professional investigation is needed. Useful maintenance information includes model details, timestamps, alarms and changes in operation. Do not clear history or reset equipment merely to obtain a simpler screenshot. Cleaning and inspection depend on the equipment and site. The assistant can explain concepts and organize questions, but cannot claim that repair, dispatch or warranty approval has occurred.

G18 — From enquiry to proposal and handover: understanding each project stage
Related topics: assessment, quotation, site visit, proposal, payment, installation, warranty; site survey, handover, project stages

FOUNDATION
A preliminary enquiry, an assessment, a written proposal, confirmed scheduling and completed handover are different stages. This chapter is an original explanation of useful project distinctions, not a newly promised Sol & Tide workflow or service deadline.

EXPLANATION AND CUSTOMER APPLICATION
The first enquiry establishes enough context to discuss the project: location, main electricity needs, backup priorities and any existing equipment. It should not require the customer to understand every technical parameter before receiving basic help.

Assessment reduces uncertainty. The team may need property and equipment information to decide what can be proposed. A preliminary calculation or package choice is not the same as confirming roof suitability, connection arrangements or all installation work.

A proposal should make the particular offer clear. Customers can ask about equipment, supported loads, scope, exclusions, assumptions, warranty responsibilities, commercial terms and the steps still requiring confirmation. The assistant should explain these categories without making up their contents.

Scheduling follows actual commitments and available arrangements. Sending a form or opening a link does not allocate equipment, reserve a date or approve a technician visit. If the customer asks whether something has been booked, the assistant must use the company's contact route rather than pretending to inspect an internal calendar.

Handover is about understanding the installed system and how to obtain support. Ask for the relevant operating information, monitoring arrangements and written records. Customer education should explain which documents to keep, while the team confirms what is actually provided.

Throughout the journey, changing requirements may change the scope. A request for another air conditioner on backup is not merely a wording edit if it alters energy and power needs. Record the change and have it reviewed instead of silently extending an old estimate.

WHAT TO CLARIFY
Identify which stage the customer is at and what evidence confirms it. Refer personal order, payment, schedule or warranty questions to the team.

SUMMARY
An enquiry, assessment, formal proposal, installation schedule and completed handover represent different project stages. Submitting a form does not confirm an appointment, and an indicative package does not establish site suitability. A quotation should identify equipment, scope, assumptions, commercial terms and unresolved matters. Changes to the customer's needs require reassessment rather than automatic extension of the original estimate.

G20 — Long-term ownership, sustainability and future changes
Related topics: lifecycle, recycling, replacement, moving, warranty transfer, future loads; warranty transfer, future demand, end of life

FOUNDATION
Solar and storage equipment has an operating life and an eventual management or replacement stage. Recovery and recycling options depend on equipment, location and available services. General environmental benefits do not establish a company take-back promise or a quantified emissions result. [D17]

EXPLANATION AND CUSTOMER APPLICATION
View the system as a long-term asset with records and responsibilities. Keep the agreed equipment and scope information, applicable warranties, support details and relevant operating history. This helps future professional work and makes it easier to distinguish an original promise from a later assumption.

Components need not have identical lifetimes or replacement conditions. A module performance warranty, an equipment defect warranty, a battery retained-capacity term and installation coverage answer different questions. The assistant should help the customer read those distinctions, not invent one universal 'system warranty'.

A change in property ownership raises questions that hardware alone cannot answer. Ask about equipment ownership, existing contracts, documentation and any transfer conditions. The presence of installed solar is not proof that every obligation or service arrangement automatically transfers.

Future needs can change. More cooling, an EV, a home office or different occupancy may affect both energy and power requirements. Expansion depends on the existing design and compatibility, not just buying a larger battery. Capture the intended change and have it assessed.

Environmental comparisons also need boundaries. A claim about avoided emissions requires a stated baseline and relevant assumptions; it should not be inferred from a generic kWh figure using an invented factor. Similarly, do not describe a product as impact-free simply because it operates without fuel combustion on the roof.

At end of life, ask qualified providers and the local team about safe handling and available options. The knowledge base does not confirm local collection services or that Sol & Tide takes back every panel or battery. Do not replace professional removal and disposal planning with a DIY procedure.

WHAT TO CLARIFY
For ownership, warranty, replacement or environmental questions, separate documented facts from assumptions and confirm the actual local service arrangements.

SUMMARY
Long-term ownership benefits from equipment, scope, warranty, support and operating records. Different components and warranties do not necessarily share the same terms. Moving home or transferring ownership does not automatically transfer every service. New loads and expansion require reassessment. Carbon estimates need a stated baseline and assumptions; recycling and replacement depend on confirmed local arrangements. Do not invent a company take-back commitment.

Definitions explain terms; they are not specifications or installation procedures.

T001 — Energy
An accumulated quantity that can be generated, transferred, stored or used; specify the time period and measurement boundary.

T002 — Electrical power
The rate of electrical energy transfer at a moment or under defined operating conditions.

T003 — Watt (W)
A unit of power; read it with the device and condition to which the rating or measurement refers.

T004 — Kilowatt (kW)
One thousand watts; used for generating, charging, discharging or load power, not stored energy.

T005 — Megawatt (MW)
One thousand kilowatts; a scale unit, not a different kind of electricity.

T006 — Watt-hour (Wh)
Energy corresponding to one watt sustained for one hour; a measure of quantity rather than instantaneous rate.

T007 — Kilowatt-hour (kWh)
One thousand watt-hours; commonly used for household consumption, generation and battery energy.

T008 — Peak rated power (kWp)
A solar power rating at reference conditions, not a guarantee of continuous roof output.

T009 — Direct current (DC)
Electrical operation with current in one direction; the DC side still needs compatible voltage, current and protection.

T010 — Alternating current (AC)
Electrical operation with periodically changing direction; household compatibility includes the relevant supply characteristics.

T011 — Voltage (V)
An electrical potential difference; matching a nominal voltage alone does not establish complete equipment compatibility.

T012 — Current (A)
The rate of electric-charge flow; a current rating requires the corresponding equipment and conditions.

T013 — Amp-hour (Ah)
A charge quantity; nominal voltage is also needed for an approximate nominal-energy calculation.

T014 — Volt-ampere (VA)
A unit of apparent power in AC equipment; do not automatically treat its numerical value as watts.

T015 — Kilovolt-ampere (kVA)
One thousand VA; compare both real-power and apparent-power constraints where specified.

T016 — Power factor
The relationship between real and apparent power under the stated conditions; relevant to some equipment ratings.

T017 — Nominal value
A stated reference or naming value, not necessarily the exact live reading or usable delivered quantity.

T018 — Rated value
A specified performance or limit under defined conditions; those conditions belong with the number.

T019 — Continuous output
Output supported over the stated operating duration and conditions, distinct from a temporary peak.

T020 — Surge or peak output
A temporary capability subject to its permitted duration and conditions, not sustained running power.

T021 — Load
An appliance or group consuming power; identify both instantaneous needs and energy over time.

T022 — Simultaneous demand
The power needed by loads operating together; different from simply adding their daily energy totals.

T023 — Duty cycle
The pattern of operation and rest or different power states; relevant when estimating energy use.

T024 — Base load
Demand that persists across much of the period being studied; its actual level is site-dependent.

T025 — Load profile
The changing demand over time, used to understand both coincidence and energy.

T026 — Essential loads
The agreed subset of appliances or circuits prioritised for backup; inclusion must be confirmed.

T027 — Runtime
The period a defined load can be supplied under stated energy, power and operating assumptions.

T028 — Autonomy
Time supported without the external source being considered; the assumed weather and starting energy matter.

T029 — Starting demand
An appliance's demand while starting, which may differ from its normal running requirement.

T030 — Cooling capacity
Thermal cooling output; not the same as an air conditioner's electrical input.

T031 — BTU per hour
A thermal rate commonly used for cooling capacity; it does not directly specify electrical consumption.

T032 — Electrical input
Electricity required by equipment under stated conditions, distinct from thermal or mechanical output.

T033 — EV charging
A household load with charger, vehicle, timing and energy requirements; backup capability is not automatic.

T034 — Storage system (BESS)
Battery storage together with management, conversion and other required equipment for the intended application.

T035 — Battery cell
An individual electrochemical unit; its characteristics are not automatically the whole system's delivered performance.

T036 — Battery module
A product-specific grouping of cells; compatibility and management depend on the equipment design.

T037 — Battery pack or bank
An assembled storage unit or supported grouping; distinguish its limits from those of one cell.

T038 — Photovoltaic (PV)
Conversion of light into electricity using photovoltaic materials and devices.

T039 — Solar thermal
Use of solar energy as heat; different equipment and output from a PV electrical system.

T040 — Solar cell
An individual photovoltaic converting element, usually packaged with others in a module.

T041 — PV module or panel
A packaged photovoltaic generating product containing cells and protective construction.

T042 — PV array
An arrangement of multiple modules forming the generating portion of a system.

T043 — Crystalline silicon
A broad photovoltaic material family; the exact module model still determines specifications.

T044 — Monocrystalline
A crystalline-material description; not a complete guarantee of yield, durability, price or suitability.

T045 — Polycrystalline
A material-structure description; compare actual products and conditions rather than the label alone.

T046 — Thin-film PV
A family using thin photovoltaic material layers; technology category does not establish an offered company product.

T047 — Bifacial module
A module designed to collect useful light from both sides; extra yield depends on rear illumination.

T048 — Tandem cell
A cell structure combining conversion layers; research capability is not proof of a quoted product's availability.

T049 — Inverter
Power-conversion equipment used to provide an appropriate AC output from a compatible DC source.

T050 — Solar inverter
An inverter serving the PV conversion function; storage or backup support must be separately established.

T051 — Battery inverter-charger
Compatible equipment for battery charging and household AC supply; product functions differ.

T052 — Hybrid inverter
Equipment combining or coordinating supported solar and battery functions; the label alone does not guarantee every backup feature.

T053 — String inverter
An inverter converting energy from groups of panels; layout and operating conditions affect the design.

T054 — Microinverter
Panel-level conversion equipment; compatibility, monitoring and costs remain model-specific.

T055 — Power optimiser
Panel-level DC control used with a compatible system; it does not create missing sunlight.

T056 — MPPT
A control process seeking a useful array operating point as electrical and environmental conditions change.

T057 — Maximum power point
An operating point on a current-voltage relationship at which available electrical power is maximised.

BOOK IV — HYPOTHETICAL WORKED CASES
============================================================================
Every input below is an explicit teaching assumption. Do not present an example as a real quotation, local rate, device rating, design, operating instruction or guaranteed outcome.

C01 — Daily energy from several loads
GIVEN
Hypothetical loads: 0.08 kW of lighting for 5 hours, 0.12 kW of electronics for 4 hours, and 0.6 kW of another load for 2 hours.

CALCULATION
Calculate each energy separately and add: 0.08 × 5 = 0.4 kWh; 0.12 × 4 = 0.48 kWh; 0.6 × 2 = 1.2 kWh. Total = 2.08 kWh.

INTERPRETATION
The energy total says nothing by itself about whether all loads run together. Their simultaneous running power would be 0.8 kW if all three were on, before any separate starting requirement.

LIMIT OF THIS EXAMPLE
Do not apply these invented appliance powers to a customer's actual equipment.

SUMMARY
Multiply each load's power by its duration, then add the results to obtain 2.08 kWh. Assess simultaneous power separately from the energy total.

C02 — Equal daily energy, different output-power needs
GIVEN
Home A uses a constant 0.5 kW for 8 hours. Home B uses 2 kW for 2 hours. Ignore all other loads and losses.

CALCULATION
Both consume 4 kWh: 0.5 × 8 = 4 and 2 × 2 = 4.

INTERPRETATION
An energy-only comparison treats the homes as equal, but the specified simultaneous demand is four times higher in B. This is why a monthly kWh total alone cannot establish inverter sizing.

LIMIT OF THIS EXAMPLE
These are intentionally simplified load profiles, not representative usage assumptions for a region.

SUMMARY
Both scenarios use 4 kWh, but the second requires a higher operating power. Monthly energy consumption alone cannot determine inverter capacity.

C03 — A defined battery energy window and delivered energy
GIVEN
A hypothetical storage reference is 10 kWh DC. The agreed example uses the window from 80% to 20% on that same reference. Conversion to the load is assumed to be 90%.

CALCULATION
Energy in the chosen window = 10 × (0.8 − 0.2) = 6 kWh DC. Delivered energy = 6 × 0.9 = 5.4 kWh.

INTERPRETATION
The calculation illustrates why a capacity label, operating window and delivered-energy boundary must be identified separately. At a constant 0.6 kW load, this modelled energy would correspond to 9 hours.

LIMIT OF THIS EXAMPLE
Do not reuse the window or efficiency as a product's actual settings, or deduct them again from a figure that already includes them.

SUMMARY
Apply the stated 80% to 20% battery window first, then the assumed efficiency, giving 5.4 kWh at the load. Do not deduct reserves or losses again if they are already included.

C04 — Available energy versus a power ceiling
GIVEN
The load-side energy available in an example is 8 kWh. A constant load asks for 4 kW, but the only supported source has a 3 kW continuous output limit.

CALCULATION
The energy-only ratio would be 8 ÷ 4 = 2 hours, but the proposed operating point fails the 3 kW power constraint.

INTERPRETATION
The two-hour arithmetic is not a feasible runtime promise under the stated source limit. A design review must address the load or supported sources before using that ratio as an outcome.

LIMIT OF THIS EXAMPLE
Do not assume the grid or another converter will supply the shortfall unless that operating arrangement exists.

SUMMARY
Although 8 kWh divided by 4 kW equals two hours, a 4 kW load exceeds the only source's 3 kW output limit. The calculation cannot support a promise of two hours of operation.

C05 — Net charging power after daytime loads
GIVEN
Solar supplies a hypothetical constant 4 kW. Loads use 1.5 kW. The charging path can accept the remaining power, and 5 kWh is required at the same energy boundary. Ignore losses.

CALCULATION
Net charging power = 4 − 1.5 = 2.5 kW. Arithmetic charging time = 5 ÷ 2.5 = 2 hours.

INTERPRETATION
Using all 4 kW as charging power would ignore the loads. A real charging estimate also needs changing sunlight, supported charging power and the relevant losses.

LIMIT OF THIS EXAMPLE
Constant power is a teaching assumption, not a forecast of two clock hours on any particular day.

SUMMARY
The 4 kW solar output supplies the 1.5 kW load first, leaving 2.5 kW for charging. Actual charging also depends on changing sunlight and charging limits.

C06 — Variable air-conditioning power over an evening
GIVEN
A hypothetical appliance averages 0.4 kW for 4 hours and 1 kW for another 2 hours.

CALCULATION
Energy = 0.4 × 4 + 1 × 2 = 3.6 kWh.

INTERPRETATION
One constant-power assumption could overstate one period and understate another. A measured or well-supported average over each period is more useful than substituting cooling capacity for electrical input.

LIMIT OF THIS EXAMPLE
The numbers are not a typical consumption figure for any air-conditioner size or a statement about overnight backup.

SUMMARY
Calculate the time intervals separately to obtain 3.6 kWh. Do not substitute cooling capacity for electrical input power or treat the example as measured consumption of a particular air conditioner.

C07 — Using an annual appliance label cautiously
GIVEN
A hypothetical label states 365 kWh per year under its defined test method.

CALCULATION
The arithmetic annual-average daily energy is 365 ÷ 365 = 1 kWh per day.

INTERPRETATION
This gives a reference average, not the consumption of every actual day. Temperature, use and operating conditions can differ, and the annual figure does not establish starting or instantaneous power.

LIMIT OF THIS EXAMPLE
Do not infer that a battery delivering 1 kWh will always support that appliance for a full day.

SUMMARY
An annual energy label can provide a reference daily average, but not a guarantee of identical daily use. It does not establish starting power or, by itself, backup duration.

C08 — Vehicle energy versus electricity required at the charging boundary
GIVEN
A hypothetical vehicle needs 10 kWh added at the defined vehicle boundary. The assumed total charging efficiency from the chosen supply boundary is 90%.

CALCULATION
Supply energy required = 10 ÷ 0.9 ≈ 11.111 kWh.

INTERPRETATION
Dividing by the fraction, rather than multiplying the requirement by it, accounts for the extra input. The result says nothing about supported charging rate or whether a home backup design includes the charger.

LIMIT OF THIS EXAMPLE
The efficiency is illustrative; use the actual vehicle, charger and measurement boundary for a project estimate.

SUMMARY
To deliver 10 kWh to the vehicle at the assumed 90% efficiency, the supply side must provide approximately 11.11 kWh. Efficiency and power limits require separate consideration.

C09 — Panel-count arithmetic is a rating, not a daily yield
GIVEN
An example array has ten modules each rated at 450 W under the same reference conditions.

CALCULATION
Rated DC capacity = 10 × 450 ÷ 1,000 = 4.5 kW.

INTERPRETATION
The result describes the sum of rated module powers. It does not state roof energy production, inverter output at every moment, battery capacity or the number of appliances that can run.

LIMIT OF THIS EXAMPLE
The example is not a Sol & Tide module or package specification.

SUMMARY
Ten 450 W modules have a combined rated capacity of 4.5 kW. This alone does not establish daily generation, backup duration or a supported appliance list.

C10 — Temperature sensitivity with an explicitly hypothetical coefficient
GIVEN
Use a hypothetical power reference of 500 W, a power coefficient of −0.3% per degree C and a 30 degree C increase relative to its reference. Hold other factors fixed.

CALCULATION
Relative change = −0.003 × 30 = −0.09. Simplified adjusted power = 500 × (1 − 0.09) = 455 W.

INTERPRETATION
The example teaches coefficient units and reference differences. It is not a complete PV model and should not be applied to another parameter such as voltage without its own coefficient.

LIMIT OF THIS EXAMPLE
Neither the coefficient nor the temperature difference is a verified specification or local weather assumption.

SUMMARY
Apply the temperature difference and coefficient to the corresponding reference quantity. The example gives 455 W. Do not apply a power coefficient indiscriminately to voltage or whole-system financial benefits.

C11 — A simplified resource-to-energy illustration
GIVEN
Use a hypothetical 4 kW rating, 4 equivalent full-sun hours and a single assumed overall energy factor of 0.8 for this example.

CALCULATION
Illustrative energy = 4 × 4 × 0.8 = 12.8 kWh.

INTERPRETATION
This is a deliberately compressed model showing why rated power, integrated resource and a stated factor are different inputs. A site forecast requires appropriate data and a clearer treatment of system conditions.

LIMIT OF THIS EXAMPLE
Four sun hours and the 0.8 factor are not published assumptions for Antigua, Dominica or a company package.

SUMMARY
The 4 kW array, four equivalent sun hours and 0.8 factor are teaching assumptions. They explain the calculation, not a local generation promise or the performance of a company package.

C12 — Sequential efficiency stages are multiplied
GIVEN
A hypothetical path has one stage at 95% efficiency followed by a different stage at 90%, with 10 kWh entering the first stage.

CALCULATION
Combined fraction = 0.95 × 0.9 = 0.855. Output = 10 × 0.855 = 8.55 kWh.

INTERPRETATION
The second stage acts on the output of the first. The calculation should not be used if a quoted round-trip figure already includes both stages, because that would count the same losses twice.

LIMIT OF THIS EXAMPLE
These assumed percentages do not describe any offered equipment.

SUMMARY
Sequential conversion stages act one after another, producing 8.55 kWh in this example. If a total efficiency already includes those stages, do not deduct them again.

C13 — Self-consumption and self-sufficiency use different denominators
GIVEN
Over the same hypothetical period without storage, PV generates 20 kWh, 12 kWh is used directly at home, and total household demand is 30 kWh.

CALCULATION
Direct solar self-consumption = 12 ÷ 20 = 60%. On-site contribution to total demand = 12 ÷ 30 = 40%.

INTERPRETATION
Both percentages can be correct because they answer different questions. The first concerns use of generation; the second concerns supply of demand. Define boundaries again if storage is included.

LIMIT OF THIS EXAMPLE
Do not describe either percentage as bill savings without the tariff and other costs.

SUMMARY
The ratio 12/20 describes the fraction of generation used on site; 12/30 describes the fraction of total demand supplied by on-site solar. Neither ratio directly equals the reduction in the electricity bill.

C14 — The incremental value of shifting surplus energy through storage
GIVEN
Hypothetically, 5 kWh would otherwise be exported at 0.10 currency units/kWh. Storage later delivers 4.5 kWh that avoids purchases at 0.30 currency units/kWh.

CALCULATION
Avoided purchase cost = 4.5 × 0.30 = 1.35. Forgone export value = 5 × 0.10 = 0.50. Difference = 0.85 currency units before other costs.

INTERPRETATION
Count the alternative use of the charging energy and the output boundary. The difference still excludes storage purchase, ageing, maintenance and any other relevant charges.

LIMIT OF THIS EXAMPLE
The example assumes export is available and gives invented prices. It does not establish a local tariff or an investment return.

SUMMARY
When assessing incremental storage benefits, account for the export value forgone and the energy actually delivered after conversion losses. The illustrative prices are not local tariffs.

C15 — A shared conversion ceiling can constrain combined sources
GIVEN
An abstract example has a shared AC output ceiling of 5 kW. The current solar contribution is 4.8 kW and household demand is 6 kW. Other limits are ignored.

CALCULATION
Headroom within the shared ceiling is 5 − 4.8 = 0.2 kW. Even if the battery could contribute more on its own, this example's combined path cannot exceed 5 kW.

INTERPRETATION
Adding independent source ratings would miss a shared bottleneck. Any remaining demand requires another supported source or a different demand arrangement; the assistant cannot infer either.

LIMIT OF THIS EXAMPLE
This is a conceptual limit example, not the wiring or operation of a specific hybrid inverter.

SUMMARY
Do not simply add the separate solar and battery output ratings. If they share a path limited to 5 kW, combined output remains subject to that limit.

C16 — Whole-project payback versus incremental battery payback
GIVEN
Hypothetical solar-only cost is 20,000 currency units with annual benefit 2,000. Solar-plus-storage cost is 30,000 with annual benefit 2,400. Hold values constant and omit other costs.

CALCULATION
Simple ratios are 20,000 ÷ 2,000 = 10 years and 30,000 ÷ 2,400 = 12.5 years. Incremental storage ratio is (30,000 − 20,000) ÷ (2,400 − 2,000) = 25 years.

INTERPRETATION
Attributing all project benefit to the battery would conceal the incremental comparison. Backup resilience is a separate customer objective that this simple money-only calculation does not value.

LIMIT OF THIS EXAMPLE
This is not a quotation, forecast, recommendation or complete investment model.

SUMMARY
Evaluate storage using its additional cost and additional benefit, rather than assigning all solar benefits to the battery. Backup value is also separate from this simple-payback calculation.

C17 — Discounting one future cash flow
GIVEN
Assume a hypothetical benefit of 1,000 currency units one year from now and an illustrative discount rate of 5%.

CALCULATION
Present value = 1,000 ÷ 1.05 ≈ 952.381 currency units.

INTERPRETATION
The example shows that cash flows at different times can be compared using an explicitly chosen financial assumption. A real analysis would need its full horizon, costs, benefits and a justified rate.

LIMIT OF THIS EXAMPLE
Five percent is not a company financing offer, a required discount rate or a prediction of inflation.

SUMMARY
The discount rate is an analytical assumption that must be stated. The example's 5% is not a financing rate, inflation forecast or recommended rate for a customer's project.

C18 — Integrating a time-based power profile
GIVEN
A hypothetical trace is exactly 1.5 kW for 2 hours and 0.5 kW for 4 hours.

CALCULATION
Energy = 1.5 × 2 + 0.5 × 4 = 5 kWh.

INTERPRETATION
This relationship holds for the defined intervals. A few screenshots taken during a day do not establish that power stayed constant between them. Meter or monitoring energy totals can provide a different kind of evidence.

LIMIT OF THIS EXAMPLE
Do not integrate sparse observations as if they were complete measured intervals.

SUMMARY
Accumulate energy by interval only when each interval's power and duration are known. A few screenshots do not establish that power remained constant between captures.

C19 — Full charge on a reduced capacity reference
GIVEN
A hypothetical battery began with a 10 kWh energy reference. For this illustration its present relevant full-capacity reference is 90% of that original value.

CALCULATION
Present full reference = 10 × 0.9 = 9 kWh.

INTERPRETATION
A display of full charge can refer to the current capacity while health compares with an earlier reference. The example does not define the actual manufacturer's SOC or SOH method.

LIMIT OF THIS EXAMPLE
Do not infer a replacement decision or warranty result from this invented percentage.

SUMMARY
A current full-charge reading and original capacity use different reference points. This example explains the distinction between SOC and health-related capacity; it cannot determine replacement or warranty eligibility.

C20 — Prioritising essential energy in a defined outage window
GIVEN
Assume 6 kWh is already available at the load, essentials need 0.25 kW for 8 hours, and an optional load would draw 1 kW. Ignore other energy use and require the separate power limit to be satisfied.

CALCULATION
Essential energy = 0.25 × 8 = 2 kWh. Remaining energy = 6 − 2 = 4 kWh, corresponding to 4 hours of the optional 1 kW load in this ideal model.

INTERPRETATION
A priority plan can distinguish essential service from discretionary use rather than assuming every load runs for the whole outage. Starting demand and simultaneous power remain separate questions.

LIMIT OF THIS EXAMPLE
The example is not an operational instruction, appliance recommendation or actual backup promise.

SUMMARY
Calculate essential-load energy first, then examine how much optional demand the remaining energy could support. Check output and starting limits separately. The example is not an actual operating plan.
